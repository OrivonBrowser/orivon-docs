## Why it exists

A web page cannot open a socket, join a peer-to-peer network, check a blockchain or keep a file on
your disk. That is the browser's sandbox doing its job. It is also why decentralised protocols
reach you through a middleman:

- the app at a `.com` is found through a registrar's DNS and served from a company's server;
- the chain is read through an RPC provider you never chose, and IPFS through a gateway that sees
  every byte;
- anything that needs a real socket becomes a download to install, update and trust on faith.

**The protocol is decentralised. The road to it is not.**

Orivon closes that gap. An app is still a web page at a link. It declares what it needs, you grant
it in plain words, and Orivon enforces your answer on every call. Names and content are proven on
your machine instead of taken on a server's word.

## Read, write, own

Web1 let you read. Web2 let you write, on someone else's servers. Web3 adds a third verb: own.
The name you open, the code you run, your keys and the decisions about them.

Orivon is a browser for that third verb. Today it opens FreeTube, Element, The Lounge, AirGap Vault
and ASGARDEX from their `.eth` names. The direction is wider: any Web3 codebase, from a full node to
a DEX to a Tor proxy, running as a site the moment you open its name.
